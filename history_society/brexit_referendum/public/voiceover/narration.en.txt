The United Kingdom joined what is now the European Union in 1973. For more than forty years, membership shaped its trade, its laws, and its politics. This is the story of how that relationship ended.

By 2015, pressure from UKIP and a rebellious backbench forced David Cameron's hand. The Conservative manifesto promised an in–out referendum. That December, Parliament passed the European Union Referendum Act, and the question was set: should the UK remain a member of the European Union, or leave it?

On the twenty-third of June, 2016, seventy-two percent of the electorate turned out, the highest for a UK-wide vote since 1992. After a campaign shadowed by the murder of Jo Cox, Leave won by fifty-two to forty-eight percent. England and Wales voted to leave; Scotland and Northern Ireland voted to remain. Cameron resigned the next morning.

What followed was three years of parliamentary deadlock. Article 50 was triggered in March 2017, starting a two-year countdown. Theresa May's deal was rejected by the largest margin in Commons history — not once, but three times. She resigned. Boris Johnson renegotiated the deal, lost every vote, and was forced to ask for an extension, before finally winning an eighty-seat majority on a promise to get Brexit done.

On the thirty-first of January, 2020, the United Kingdom formally left the European Union. But leaving was only the start: eleven months later, just as the transition expired, the two sides signed a thin trade deal — no tariffs, but new borders, new paperwork, and a permanently changed relationship.

Brexit reshaped British politics for a generation. It settled one question — membership — and opened many more: about the union itself, about trade, and about who gets to decide. The process, from pledge to exit, took less than five years. Its consequences are still unfolding.
